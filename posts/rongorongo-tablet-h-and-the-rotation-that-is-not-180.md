# rongorongo tablet H and the rotation that is not 180

Back to Rongorongo. Not the Staff this time. Tablet H, also called the Great Santiago Tablet. Museo Nacional de Historia Natural, Santiago, catalogue number 316.

Boustrophedon reverse. The standard description: read line 1 left to right, rotate the tablet 180 degrees, read line 2 left to right, rotate again. Fourteen lines on the recto, twelve on the verso. The signs on even lines are inverted relative to the signs on odd lines, which is what makes the rotation legible as rotation.

Steven Fischer measured the sign inversions in 1997. Most pairs invert cleanly. Seven signs on tablet H do not. They are rotated, but not by 180 degrees. Fischer gives the deviation as "between 7 and 12 degrees off axis." He attributes it to carving error.

Thomas Barthel, working from the Berlin rubbings in 1958, flagged the same seven signs. He does not call it error. His note reads: "the hand rotates less than the eye expects."

The seven anomalous signs on tablet H are all the same glyph. Barthel catalogues it as 076. Fischer catalogues it as 076. Pozdniakov's 2011 statistical corpus lists 076 as one of the twenty most frequent signs on the tablets. On tablet H it appears 23 times. Sixteen of those occurrences invert at 180. Seven do not.

I pulled the Jaussen list photographs from the 1893 plates. The seven off-axis occurrences cluster on lines 3, 5, 7, 9, 11, 13 of the recto, and line 2 of the verso. One per odd recto line, plus the single verso instance.

Position on the line varies. Line 3: seventh sign from the left end. Line 5: seventh from the left end. Line 7: seventh from the left end. Lines 9, 11, 13 all identical. Line 2 of the verso: seventh from the right end, which, after rotation, is seventh from the left in the reading direction.

Fischer's 1997 monograph does not mention the position regularity. I went back through it twice. Appendix B gives line-by-line sign positions for tablet H but does not tabulate 076 separately. The regularity is visible in his own data.

The sign 076 is one Barthel gloss calls a "bent arm." Métraux's 1940 Easter Island ethnography lists a gesture used in recitation of genealogies: the left arm bent at the elbow, rotated outward from the body at roughly ten degrees from the vertical. The gesture marks the point at which the reciter pauses and begins the next generation.

Barthel does not cite Métraux. Métraux does not cite Barthel. They worked in the same decade on the same island and did not cross-reference.

Tablet H is one of the three tablets Metoro Tauara chanted for Bishop Jaussen in 1873. Metoro's reading of H is the longest he gave. The transcription runs to forty-two pages in Jaussen's notebook. Jaussen's cover page gives the duration of the recitation as "approximately seven hours, with pauses."

The number of pauses is not recorded.

I counted the 076 signs across the three Metoro tablets. H: 23. Aruku Kurenga: 14. Mamari: 7. The corpus is too small to call this a pattern. I counted them again anyway.

The Santiago museum catalogue entry for tablet H was updated in March 2009. The update field reads only "image corrected." The pre-2009 image is no longer on the public server. A mirror at the University of Chile preserves it. On the pre-2009 image, one of the seven off-axis 076 signs on line 7 appears to carry a small dot inside the curve of the bent arm. On the current image the dot is not visible.
